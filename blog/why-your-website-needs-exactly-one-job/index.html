Why your website needs exactly one job

Most sites try to do everything and end up doing nothing well. Here is how to find the one job yours should do.

Open ten competitor homepages in your industry and you will see the same pattern: a hero slider cycling through five different messages, a nav bar with eleven items, a page trying to be a brochure, a resume, a store and a blog all at once. None of it is badly designed in isolation. It is badly prioritized, and prioritization, not decoration, is most of what separates a site that converts from one that just sits there.

Start with the one sentence a visitor needs to act on

Every page that works has an answer to one question: what is the single next action a visitor should take, and what is the minimum they need to believe before they take it. For a dental implant clinic, that is book a consultation, and the belief required is this is a credible, safe procedure. Everything else, the awards, the technology explainer, the founder bio, exists to support that one belief, not to compete with it.

When a site has five jobs instead of one, visitors do not do all five. They do the one that is easiest to find, or they do none, because nothing on the page makes the decision for them.

The test: delete sections until it breaks

A useful exercise is to go through your homepage section by section and ask: if I deleted this, would the one action get weaker? If the answer is no, the section is there for a reason that is not the visitor’s, usually because someone internally wanted it included. That is not a design problem, it is a scoping problem, and it has to be solved before a single pixel gets designed.

  • If a section does not move someone closer to the one action, cut it or move it to a secondary page.
  • If a section repeats a claim already made elsewhere, merge it.
  • If a section exists because a competitor has it, that is the weakest reason to keep anything.

One job does not mean one page

This is not an argument for a single-page site with nothing on it. A site can have a blog, a pricing page, a careers page, plenty of pages, each doing its own one job. The mistake is cramming all of those jobs onto the page that gets the most traffic, usually the homepage, because it feels efficient. It is not. A homepage that tries to be the pricing page, the portfolio and the contact form at once ends up being a worse version of all three.

We build this site the same way: the homepage’s one job is to show you that your site could be losing you customers, and what that looks like fixed. Everything after that, the work, the packages, this post, lives on its own page, doing its own job.
